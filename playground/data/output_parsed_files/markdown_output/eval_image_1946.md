

## No longer a labour cost issue
France's weak export competitiveness can no longer be exclusively attributed to labour costs. Indeed, France's relative unit labour costs within the eurozone went back to their 1996 levels just before the pandemic, without an improvement in export market shares.

This confirms that the country needs to improve its non-cost competitiveness situation, in our view, through innovation and increased quality of products.

## Evolution, not revolution
Prime Minister Attal intends to continue structural reforms, especially in the labour market. One target is to raise the senior employment rate following the pension reform enacted last year. Another goal is to raise incentives to work through tighter eligibility conditions to receive unemployment benefits.

## Long list of French labour market measures
French labour reforms since 2015 have been comprehensive, but, unlike some peers, have not introduced more flexible employment contracts (see Figure 34).

The main measures enacted include:

* easier negotiations between unions and companies at the company level;
* lower labour taxes;
* a cap on fines for unfair dismissals;
* quicker processes for collective dismissals;
* declining unemployment benefits in good economic times;
* requiring welfare benefit recipients to undertake some economic activity;
* large subsidies for apprenticeships; and
* the creation of a universal personal account for continuous education.

## Committed to reform
While French labour reforms have been comprehensive thus far, they have not yet directly adjusted the minimum wage or introduced more flexibility to contracts. With regard to the former, the government intervened indirectly through the tax wedge. By cutting costs for employers without changing the minimum wage, the reform contributed to making labour costs more competitive over time.

Overall, the government's commitment to labour market reforms has now spanned nearly a decade, and there appears to be substantial political will to carry on.
