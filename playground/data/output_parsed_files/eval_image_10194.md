

## Potential worries ahead:
As neither we nor international organisations expect France's public debt ratio to rise much in the coming years, we do not consider its fiscal situation overly worrying yet.

However, the stabilisation of the debt ratio depends on some fiscal effort in the years ahead, which, given the political situation, may be difficult to achieve.

In addition, as debt dynamics in peripheral economies have become healthier than in the previous few decades, the French fiscal situation appears less positive from a relative perspective.

Finally, with a public debt ratio of 110% of GDP, France has less leeway to absorb a new economic shock than it did before the pandemic. If another significant shock were to occur, the government might not be able to protect consumers and businesses in the same way it has over past few years.

## Long period of fiscal consolidation:
Overall, we estimate that France needs to cut its structural deficit by 2.2% of GDP from 2023 to 2028 to stabilise the public debt ratio. This represents an annual effort of 0.4–0.5% of GDP.

This is unlikely to be the end of the consolidation. The recent compromise around EU budget rules includes a safeguard that requires member states to cut the debt ratio by 0.5pp or 1pp per year, depending on the starting level of debt-to-GDP.

Fiscal consolidation of 2.2% appears feasible in the context of France's past efforts. However, the 2010–15 fiscal consolidation of 3.3% of GDP led to austerity fatigue and some political instability.

## Optimistic forecasts:
The French government has missed its two-year ahead deficit forecasts every year since 2001, with a median miss of 1.9% of GDP. Admittedly, since the pandemic, its current-year and year-ahead forecasts have been conservative enough to deliver positive surprises.

## Poor results of past spending reviews:
The French government committed to find EUR12bn structural savings for 2025, on top of new EUR10bn savings for 2024. In the past two decades, France committed to spending reviews three times: the RGPP under President Nicolas Sarkozy (2007), MAP under President Hollande (2012) and Action Publique 2022 under President Macron (2017). The results of these spending reviews have been unimpressive. Government spending as a share of GDP has continued to rise, with no large structural savings found.
