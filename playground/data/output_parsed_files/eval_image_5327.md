

**Selective outperformance:** Since the start of the war in Ukraine, the French economy has outperformed the eurozone – a bright spot in the core, particularly relative to Germany. From Q2 2022 to Q4 2023, the country grew by 1.3% against 0.5% for the eurozone and -0.2% for Germany. Nevertheless, some European countries fared much better during the same period: GDP in Spain rose by 3.1%.

Questions remain, though, on whether there is scope for this selective outperformance to continue or whether the looming challenges are insurmountable. Prime Minister Gabriel Attal took office last month with a continued commitment to reform; but though we see that as a step in the right direction, it is uncertain to what extent his government's policy decisions will impact the macro outlook (for more on this, see page 15).

**Limited exposure to energy and trade shock:** In our view, the predominant reason for France's better performance has been its relatively low exposure to the energy shock. Energy-intensive industries have a share of 3.6% in overall value-added in the country, compared with 6.4% in Germany and 5.8% in Italy.

As France is typically less sensitive to external demand, it was also less exposed than its European peers to the c.2% contraction in global trade in 2023.

**Resilient disposable incomes:** Another factor that explains French growth performance is government support. Following the energy crisis, gas and electricity prices for households were frozen between October 2021 and February 2023. Combined with generous support during the Covid-19 pandemic period, household disposable income in France was 4.0% above its 2019 level in Q3 2023, substantially above the eurozone average of only 2.7% for the same period, emphasising the impact of the government's generous policy response.

**Meagre growth in Q4:** Over recent quarters and alongside the withdrawal of government support, growth in France has moderated. According to the second estimate, French GDP rose 0.1% q/q Q4, leaving annual growth at 0.9% in 2023. Concerningly, domestic demand was weak during the quarter, with contractions in investment, imports, and unchanged consumption.

Going into 2024, this leaves carryover at just 0.2%, making the government's new GDP growth forecast of 1.0% still optimistic, requiring a significant pickup in activity.
