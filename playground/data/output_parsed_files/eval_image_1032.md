

**Low approval ratings:** President Macron's approval ratings are at historically low levels (24%), not far from the 21% recorded at the peak of the 'yellow vest' crisis in 2018. According to recent polls, his party is also trailing the far-right party National Rally by almost 10pt (19% versus 28%). Against this backdrop, painful reforms could be difficult to pass.

The government might also yield to further sector-specific demands. For example, recent protests by farmers have resulted in EUR400mn new spending.

**No absolute majority:** The government does not have an absolute majority in the National Assembly, so it has to negotiate with other parties to pass bills. For important reforms (up to one per year) and the budget, the government can use the Article 49.3 of the Constitution. This article allows a reform to pass without a vote unless a no-confidence vote is adopted.

French voters appear dissatisfied with this situation, with falling trust in the parliament.

**High political polarisation:** The French political system has become more polarised in recent years, with the rise of populist parties from both the left and the right and a decline of traditional parties. According to a study of historical episodes of fiscal consolidation (Eichengreen and Panizza, 2016), the presence of a divided government is a statistically significant factor that reduces the likelihood of a sustained primary surplus.
